An atlas of architectural exceptions

When 60 floors beat 102.

New York’s 270 Park Avenue is taller than the Empire State Building—despite having 42 fewer floors. What happens when we put thousands of towers on the same graph?

A floor is not a unit of height.

We assume more floors mean a taller building. Usually, they do. But the interesting stories live in the exceptions: enormous offices, thin residential stacks, gardens in the sky, and pinnacles built to be seen rather than occupied.

01 / THE PARADOX

Not all 60th floors are equal.

New York’s 270 Park Avenue climbs 423 metres in 60 stories. The Empire State Building needs 102 stories to reach 381 metres. Both numbers are real; neither tells you how much usable space is inside.

02 / THE CROWN

A taller peak. Fewer floors.

Merdeka 118 reaches 679 metres with 118 floors; Shanghai Tower reaches 632 metres with 128. A spire can change the ranking without giving anyone an extra office. Height and occupied height are not synonyms.

03 / THE EXCEPTION

A hotel with gardens in the sky.

Singapore’s Oasia Hotel Downtown is roughly 193 metres high but has just 27 above-ground floors. Its 30-metre-high sky gardens show how a building can spend height on air, not on another numbered level.

ANOTHER WAY TO SEE THE DATA

An office floor is a different kind of floor.

Across the buildings with a stated use, office towers have a higher median metres-per-reported-floor ratio than residential towers. The difference holds within every one of 26 city-and-floor-range groups with at least five of each use. This is not a measured ceiling height: the ratio also counts crowns, ground floors and gaps.

How to read this

Horizontal: reported above-ground floors. Vertical: reported height in metres, generally to the architectural top, which can include spires.

Outlier score: actual height minus 25.6 + 3.44 × floors. This robust straight line is fitted once to the full sample, not refitted as filters change. It is a comparison device, not a building design rule.

Sample, not a census

Data extracted 6 November 2026 from the main tall-buildings tables of 41 English Wikipedia city pages, with 47 distinct city labels including places listed in a national table. Filters retain buildings ≥100 m, ≥18 floors and reported completion years no later than 2026 (or unknown).

City tables differ in completeness, use category and whether topped-out buildings are included. About half lack a stated building use. This sample cannot rank the world’s cities or estimate global building prevalence. Two observation towers and four rows with implausibly low height/floor ratios were excluded during cleaning.

The measurement trap

Reported floors are not uniformly counted: marketing, skipped numbers and mechanical floors can disagree. For example, One World Trade Center appears with 104 in one Wikipedia NYC table, but the building-focused CTBUH record reports 94. These differences change an apparent outlier.
